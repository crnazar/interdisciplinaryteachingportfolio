Teaching Statement

Description before evaluation

On translating intuitive fluency into systematic critical analysis — and on teaching with, and against, the machine.

My professional career spans elementary, secondary, and postsecondary education, with a particular focus on university instruction in education and the STEM disciplines. STEM education has consistently been a foundational pillar of my scholarly and instructional focus. Throughout my career I have remained dedicated to investigating how people perceive their environments, and how empirical evidence drawn from those interactions can be leveraged to improve societal outcomes and support local communities. Although I initially pursued a Ph.D. in teacher education to refine how educators analyze student performance data, that work fostered an unexpected and profound commitment to the ontological and philosophical frameworks underpinning pedagogical practice. This evolution led me to complete a second Ph.D. in the digital humanities, in the Texts & Technology program. As my alma mater, the University of Central Florida offered a transformative environment that enabled me to succeed as a minoritized scholar and to address the questions that continue to inform my research and teaching.

While students in media and cultural studies often arrive deeply familiar with the cultural artifacts we examine, they frequently lack the methodological training to use that familiarity as rigorous analytical evidence. My pedagogy translates intuitive fluency into systematic critical analysis by cultivating three intellectual habits. First, I enforce a strict standard of description before evaluation: students must produce comprehensive, objective descriptions of an artifact before advancing a thesis, because superficial interpretations usually stem from inadequate close analysis rather than theoretical deficiency. Second, students learn to contextualize objects within larger systemic frameworks — the hosting platforms, financial structures, and archival practices (and omissions) that shape them. Third, I emphasize active production: because critical analysis and creative execution rely on complementary cognitive faculties, students who build digital artifacts develop a far more sophisticated perspective when evaluating the work of others.

These principles are synthesized in a course of my own design, Encountering the World, through its primary assessment, “The Field Log and Its Double.” Students keep an empirical observational log of a site they visit repeatedly across the term, prioritizing rigorous description over immediate interpretation. In the latter phase of the course, they generate a machine-encoded counterpart by prompting a computational model to construct its own account of the site from their field notes. The core exercise is comparative: students evaluate what the model reproduces accurately, what it homogenizes, and what its apparent fluency obscures. Weekly log submissions and a mid-term analytical essay keep the interpretive process transparent and assessable well before the final artifact is complete.

This scaffolding was a deliberate response to challenges I identified in an earlier iteration of the course, in which the entire project was collected only at the end of the term. That first approach yielded highly polished final products, but it hid the thinking that mattered most — and it taught me how to refine the course to better align with contemporary digital-humanities scholarship. My aim now is for students to engage user interfaces contemplatively, building a critical understanding of the physical world that extends beyond passive reliance on AI systems projected to dominate future knowledge industries. Consequently, the analytical argument must be finalized before technical construction, so the final build stays accountable to the student's own theoretical framework. The field log is the definitive test of the method: the primary record must reflect the student's direct observations, because the computational double can only be evaluated against an account verified by human perception.

How the course developed

An earlier version of Encountering the World collected the whole project only at the end of term. It produced polished artifacts — but the interpretive thinking stayed hidden. The current design scaffolds the work with weekly field logs and a mid-term essay, so the argument is finalized before the machine account is ever generated.

Although my framework incorporates digital methods, computational tools support scholarly inquiry rather than serve as an end in themselves. I design courses on the assumption that mine may be a student's only exposure to digital methods; I assume no prior technical proficiency, and I never let technical instruction supersede pedagogical substance. In Encountering the World, students use web archiving, corpus linguistics, network visualization, and collaborative digital annotation not merely to compile data but to make complex interpretive questions analytically manageable. I explicitly name the transferability of these competencies — data structuring, archival curation, provenance assessment, public-facing argumentation — because humanities students are too often told their training lacks commercial viability.

For a department weighing how to integrate digital methods, my proposal is not an isolated digital concentration. Rather, I address traditional disciplinary questions — representation, circulation, power, and audience — by expanding the scope of admissible evidence. Digital assignments are treated as exercises in interpretive knowledge production and assessed by rigorous academic standards. When student projects are designed for public distribution, I address intellectual property and labor directly in the syllabus: students retain authorship, public dissemination requires explicit consent rather than assumption, and the scope of the project is calibrated to the assigned credit hours. Public-facing work heightens motivation precisely because its significance exceeds the classroom — and that same quality demands careful ethical oversight against the exploitation of student labor.

Over the past year I have run intentional pedagogical experiments with generative AI. In my designs, AI is permitted for scaffolding — brainstorming, generating counterarguments to test student claims, drafting boilerplate code, and clarifying technical terms — but prohibited for the competencies I formally assess: the primary description of textual or cultural objects, the formulation of interpretive arguments, and peer feedback. Description and interpretation are the primary sites of learning; outsourcing them compromises the coursework's integrity. Peer evaluation is restricted because its value is social and collaborative, not merely textual. To ensure accountability, every major assignment requires a process note documenting the tools used, the queries submitted, and the outputs kept or rejected. That note is graded, shifting the classroom from technological surveillance to explicit methodology and fostering metacognition more effectively than a standard reflection prompt.

Within a media-studies frame, AI systems are also primary objects of critical inquiry. Students analyze them through the same lenses they apply to other media infrastructures: the labor conditions of training, data exploitation and intellectual property, corporate profit structures, and the systematic visibility or invisibility of particular algorithmic outputs. We study institutional proposals for how libraries, archives, and museums might engage AI on self-determined terms — exposing collections selectively while directing users to original sources — alongside critical evaluations of whether these institutions have historically participated in exploitative data practices. Students who wrestle with these arguments develop the capacity to make informed professional decisions about tool use, moving beyond passive compliance with arbitrary rules.

Across every context, my objective remains constant: to cultivate students who can isolate a contemporary media artifact, subject it to rigorous description, and formulate verifiable, critical truths about its function in society.
