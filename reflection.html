Final Reflection

What the machine cannot cross — on building this portfolio, and on how a semester of digital-humanities pedagogy sharpened where I draw the line with AI.

When this semester began, I already thought of myself as someone who taught with technology, a conviction rooted in my extensive experience across multiple educational tiers spanning elementary, secondary, and postsecondary levels, with a particular focus on university instruction within education and STEM disciplines. Throughout my career, STEM education has consistently served as a foundational pillar of my scholarly and instructional focus, dedicated to investigating how individuals perceive their environments and how empirical evidence derived from these interactions can be leveraged to improve societal outcomes and support local communities. I was especially concerned with how technology would be used to teach future teachers and how we can uphold student and community knowledges through technology. Although I initially pursued a Ph.D. in teacher education to refine the methodologies educators use to analyze student performance data, this endeavor fostered an unexpected and profound commitment to the ontological and philosophical frameworks underpinning pedagogical practices, leading to a second Ph.D. within the digital humanities, specializing in the Texts & Technology program at the University of Central Florida. What I could not yet do before this term was say why with any precision, or draw a defensible line between the work I wanted machines to do and the work I think they shouldn't do. The readings this term, and the experience of building this portfolio, did not overturn my convictions so much as sharpen them into something I can now teach, providing a scholarly warrant to translate my intuitive pedagogical approach into a systematic framework.

That border — what the machine cannot cross — is where I intend to focus my work in the future.

The first shift came from reading digital humanities pedagogy as an argument about liberal education. Locke (2017) reframed the digital not as a set of competencies in a course but as a way of making old interpretive questions — about power, representation, and evidence — newly askable. That framing gave me language for something I had been doing intuitively in my new goal of developing the course, Encountering the World. This work came from the feedback given throughout the semester and the deep focus on understanding exactly how my previous work fit within the digital humanities and how I can build on this work moving forward by creating either interdisciplinary or transdisciplinary lenses. Specifically, this course allows students to utilize web archiving, corpus linguistics, network visualization, and collaborative digital annotation not merely to compile data, but to render complex interpretive questions analytically manageable. Rather than treating technology as a technical hurdle, I teach these digital tools because they make a challenging interpretive problem analytically manageable within a humanistic framework. Ultimately, engaging with Locke (2017) let me see it as humanistic inquiry and learning through that lens.

Brier and Wilner (2018) and understanding the habit of judgment reshaped how I sequence my seminar. It is the reason I built an explicit “Teaching for Transfer” unit and a Transfer Plan deliverable into the syllabus, asking students to name how a method they practiced on one artifact would travel to their own field. Reading Brier and Wilner (2018), I realized that transfer is not a bonus at the end of a course; it is the thing being taught, and it must be scaffolded as deliberately as any content.

Martin (2024) deepened my sense that making is itself a form of humanistic argument. The command line is treated as a site where humanists confront the assumptions built into their tools. This resonated with my conviction that critical analysis and creative production draw on complementary faculties — that students who build a digital artifact understand its politics far better than those who only critique one. Martin (2024) gave me a scholarly basis for treating a student's build as evidence of interpretation.

The readings that most changed my position, though, were those on AI specifically. Cohen offered a model for engagement in which cultural institutions selectively expose their collections to AI on self-determined terms, while directing users back to original sources. That posture is the one I now want my students to take. It is why my courses treat AI as an object of critique, not merely as a productivity aid. Cohen helped me see that the ethical question is that teaching students to set those terms is a genuine intellectual skill.

Mollick is blunt that if an assignment can be completed invisibly by a chatbot, the assignment — not the student — is the problem. Rather than treating that as a threat, I have used it to clarify what I actually assess: the competencies at the center of my grading. My process-note requirement, in which students document the tools they used and the outputs they kept or rejected, is my answer to Mollick, making AI use itself an object of metacognition.

All of this converged in the act of building this portfolio. I made it through an agentic AI workflow — planning the structure and aesthetic in advance, giving specific guidance on color and type, and iterating in conversation before a single page was deployed to GitHub Pages. The experience was an unusually clean demonstration of my own pedagogy. The machine was genuinely useful for scaffolding, drafting, and construction; it was useless for deciding what the portfolio should mean. I had to hold the argument steady while it built.

So my position on AI in the classroom has not reversed, but needs to include the embodied, situated knowing that is the real curriculum. My students will use these tools out loud, disclose them, and turn them into objects of study. And they will keep a primary record verified by their own perception. Taking from Anzaldúa, I think my dissertation will discuss this borderland. That border — what the machine cannot cross — is where I intend to focus my work in the future.
